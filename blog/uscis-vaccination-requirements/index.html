Vaccinations

USCIS vaccination requirements, and what happens if records are missing

There is no single list that applies to everyone. What you need depends on your age and your immunization history, and two of the requirements changed recently.

Reviewed 2026-09-08· 3 min read· NY MedCare, Manhattan

At a glance

  • Requirements follow the CDC immunization schedule and depend on your age.
  • Polio (IPV) was added to the technical instructions for civil surgeons in May 2024.
  • The COVID-19 requirement was removed by USCIS effective 22 January 2025.
  • Lost records are common and not a dead end — blood titers can document existing immunity.

How the requirements are set

USCIS does not publish one flat list of vaccines that every applicant needs. Requirements follow the CDC immunization schedule, and which ones apply to you depends on your age and your existing immunization history. A four-year-old and a forty-five-year-old are assessed against different parts of the same schedule.

This is the part most applicants find hardest to work out on their own, and it is the part a civil surgeon does for you at the visit.

The vaccinations that commonly apply

Depending on your age and history, these are the ones that come up:

  • MMR (measles, mumps, rubella)
  • Tdap / Td
  • Polio (IPV)
  • Varicella (chickenpox)
  • Hepatitis A
  • Hepatitis B
  • Haemophilus influenzae type b
  • Rotavirus
  • Meningococcal
  • Pneumococcal
  • Seasonal influenza

Not all of these will apply to you. Several are specific to infants and young children.

Two changes worth knowing about

Polio (IPV) was added to the CDC technical instructions for civil surgeons in May 2024. If you are working from older guidance or an older checklist, it may not appear there.

The COVID-19 vaccination requirement was removed by USCIS effective 22 January 2025. You no longer need to document it on Form I-693. Applicants who prepared their paperwork before that change are often still carrying documentation they do not need.

If you have lost your vaccination records

This is one of the most common situations we see, and it is not a dead end. Depending on your age and history there are two routes:

  • Repeat the vaccines. For several vaccines, simply receiving the doses again is safe and straightforward, and it documents the requirement cleanly.
  • Order blood titers. A titer is a blood test that measures whether you are already immune. If it shows immunity, that satisfies the requirement without repeating the vaccine.

Either route completes the vaccination section of your form. Which one makes sense is a clinical judgement made at your visit, not something you need to decide in advance.

Records from another country

Bring them, however old they are and whatever language they are in. Childhood records from your country of origin are frequently usable, and they can save you both doses and money. If they turn out to be incomplete, titers remain available as a fallback.

Where the prescriptions come from

At your appointment the civil surgeon identifies exactly which vaccinations are outstanding for your age and writes the prescriptions, so you can complete them at your own pharmacy rather than making a second clinical visit.

Questions we get about this

Do I still need to document a COVID-19 vaccination?

No. USCIS removed the COVID-19 vaccination requirement effective 22 January 2025, so it no longer needs to be recorded on Form I-693.

Are vaccination records in another language acceptable?

Bring them. Childhood records from your country of origin are frequently usable, whatever language they are in and however old they are. If they turn out to be incomplete, blood titers remain available as a fallback.

How long do titer results take?

Titers are processed by the same outside laboratory as the rest of your required testing, so expect the same 3 to 5 business day turnaround.
